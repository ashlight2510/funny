Spread of Major Livestock Infectious Diseases… Surge in Livestock Prices

Recently, the simultaneous spread of three major livestock infectious diseases—highly pathogenic avian influenza (AI), African swine fever (ASF), and foot-and-mouth disease—has led to a sharp rise in livestock prices. As a result, the prices of various livestock products such as chicken, beef, and pork have increased by an average of 6% compared to a year ago, with the price of a dozen eggs nearing 7,000 won. The Ministry of Agriculture, Food and Rural Affairs has announced that all these diseases have reached a serious stage and has extended special biosecurity measures for another month.

This situation is not just limited to price increases; it is also causing instability in the supply and demand of livestock products. In particular, large-scale culling of chickens and pigs has led to a sharp decline in production, further exacerbating the price increases felt by consumers.

Key Summary

  • Simultaneous occurrence of three major livestock infectious diseases causing a surge in livestock prices.
  • Egg prices have risen by 6.7% compared to a year ago, nearing 7,000 won per dozen.
  • Prices of pork and beef have also increased by 7.3% and 5.6%, respectively.
  • The Ministry of Agriculture, Food and Rural Affairs has extended special biosecurity measures to prevent disease spread.
  • Disruption in livestock supply affecting consumer prices.

Key Scenes

  • Simultaneous outbreaks of highly pathogenic AI, ASF, and foot-and-mouth disease leading to biosecurity emergencies.
  • The rate of increase in livestock prices exceeding the overall consumer price inflation rate.
  • Decrease in production due to large-scale culling.
  • Increased burden on consumers' living expenses.
  • Continued potential for instability in livestock supply in the future.

Why Background Context is Important

The simultaneous occurrence of these livestock infectious diseases is an unprecedented phenomenon not seen from 2019 to 2024, with occurrences happening for two consecutive years. Notably, the number of cases of highly pathogenic AI has increased to 32 last year and 56 this year, while ASF has recorded the highest number of cases in history. These diseases are classified as first-class livestock infectious diseases due to their rapid transmission and significant damage, necessitating urgent strengthening of biosecurity systems.

Why It Matters

The surge in livestock prices does not merely impose an economic burden on consumers. It can lead to an overall increase in food prices, significantly impacting low-income households. Particularly, rising prices of essential foods can hit low-income groups hard. Moreover, if the livestock industry suffers extensive damage, it could have long-term negative effects on the rural economy.

Therefore, effective government measures are necessary, and consumers should also be aware of and prepare for this situation.

Hidden Variables to Consider

An aspect not mentioned in this article is the long-term economic repercussions of the spread of livestock infectious diseases. It could affect not only the livestock industry but also related industries such as feed manufacturers and distributors, potentially shaking the entire food system. Additionally, it should not be overlooked that consumers seeking alternative food products may also influence the prices of other food categories.

FAQ

A: The continued rise in livestock prices may increase the burden on consumers' living expenses. Additionally, if the livestock industry suffers, it will negatively impact the rural economy.

Q: What measures is the government implementing?

Q: What should consumers pay attention to?
A: Consumers should closely monitor changes in living expenses due to rising livestock prices and consider adjusting their consumption patterns or seeking alternative food products.

In this situation, the most important thing is to continuously monitor the effectiveness of the government's biosecurity measures and for consumers to respond sensitively to price fluctuations.
