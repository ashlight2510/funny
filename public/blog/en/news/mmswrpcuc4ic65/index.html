Former Commanders Charged with Insurrection: Key Points to Consider

March 16, 2026 · 경향신문 · View source

Original Article at a Glance Title: Former commanders involved in 'insurrection' deny all charges, claiming they were just following orders as soldiers Source: Kyunghyang Shinmun Former commanders facing trial for charges related to the December 3 insurrection appeared for their first trial at the Seoul Central District Court on the 16th. According to current law, trials for active-duty soldiers are held in military courts, but they have been dismissed by the Ministry of National Defense and are now being tried in civilian courts. The 26th Criminal Division of the Seoul Central District Court (presided over by Lee Hyun-kyung) heard the case of former Army Intelligence Command Chief Yeo In-hyung, former Capital Defense Command Chief Lee Jin-woo, and others. Former Commanders Deny Charges of Insurrection, Claiming They Were Just Following Orders

Recently, former military commanders have come under scrutiny as they face trial for charges of insurrection. They are accused of supporting former President Yoon Suk-yeol's plan to declare a state of emergency and mobilizing military forces to execute it. However, they assert that they were merely following orders from their superiors and deny all allegations. Notably, only former Commander Kwak has admitted to his charges, while the other commanders claim they had no intention of violating the constitution.

This case has sparked a profound discussion about political responsibility and the role of the military, going beyond mere compliance with military orders. Questions are being raised about the legal and ethical boundaries when soldiers carry out political orders and the potential social ramifications that may arise from such actions.

Why Background Matters

This case is related to the December 3 insurrection incident, where military commanders are accused of supporting former President Yoon Suk-yeol's declaration of a state of emergency. They are reported to have executed orders to block the National Assembly and arrest politicians. This raises the need for discussions about the legal and ethical boundaries when soldiers carry out political orders. Notably, while trials for soldiers are typically held in military courts, these individuals transitioned to civilian status and are now being tried in civilian courts.

Why It Matters

This case goes beyond the punishment of a few soldiers; it deeply addresses the relationship between the military and politics, as well as the responsibilities of soldiers. When soldiers execute political orders, there is a need to determine whether those orders are legally justified, which could set important precedents for future military orders. Additionally, this case raises concerns about trust in the military and issues of political neutrality.

Personal Perspective

In my view, this case illustrates how blurred the lines can be between military duty and political orders. Soldiers have an obligation to follow orders, but how should the legality of those orders be judged? There needs to be a discussion about how responsibilities should be divided when soldiers execute political commands. If soldiers increasingly face punishment for following orders, it could negatively impact military morale and performance.

Hidden Variables to Consider

A critical point in this case is the ambiguity surrounding the legal standards when soldiers execute orders. In situations where soldiers find it difficult to make legal judgments, they may face punishment for following orders, complicating the relationship between the military and politics. This poses a risk of undermining trust in soldiers' execution of orders.

Current Points to Monitor

  • It is essential to closely observe how the progress of this case and the trial outcomes will impact future military-political relations.
  • Attention should be paid to how discussions regarding the legal standards for executing military orders unfold.
