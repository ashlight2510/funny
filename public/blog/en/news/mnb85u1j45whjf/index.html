“Hair designers are also workers”… The Labor Commission recognizes it following the Supreme Court

Recently, a series of rulings recognizing the worker status of hair designers have heralded significant changes in employment practices within the beauty industry. The Chungnam Regional Labor Commission recognized hair designers as workers in the case of intern A's application for remedy against unfair dismissal, opening the door for beauty industry workers to assert their legal rights. This is not limited to a single case but reveals structural issues throughout the entire beauty industry.

This ruling follows the Supreme Court's decision, and it is significant in that hair designers have been recognized as workers entitled to the protections of the Labor Standards Act, rather than as independent business operators. Notably, this ruling puts a stop to the practice of hair salons attempting to evade the application of the Labor Standards Act through 'business splitting'.

Key Summary

  • The Supreme Court and the local labor commission recognize hair designers as workers.
  • A halt to the practice of 'business splitting' by hair salons.
  • Increased possibility for workers to assert their rights, providing an opportunity to address unfair dismissal issues.
  • Originated from intern A's case of application for remedy against unfair dismissal.

Key Highlights

  • Recognition of Worker Status: Hair designers are judged not to be independent business operators but workers.
  • Recognition of Unfair Dismissal: Intern A's dismissal is deemed unfair.
  • Issue of Business Splitting: The act of registering two hair salons as separate businesses is judged illegal.
  • Expansion of Labor Standards Act Protection: Workers in the beauty industry can now assert rights such as annual leave and severance pay.
  • Strengthening Legal Responsibility: There is a possibility of criminal liability for employers who fail to pay severance to workers.

Why Background Matters

This case arises from the long-standing practices of 'fake 3.3' and 'business splitting' in the beauty industry. The attempts by hair salons to operate two separate businesses to evade the application of the Labor Standards Act have ultimately been dismantled by legal judgment. Such rulings are not confined to a single salon's case but serve as a catalyst for re-examining worker rights across the beauty industry.

Why It Matters

The recognition of hair designers as workers opens opportunities for better working conditions for those in the beauty industry. It raises awareness among employers who have illegally evaded worker status and establishes a path for workers to receive legal protection. Ultimately, this is expected to enhance fairness in the labor market and foster an environment where workers can assert their rights.

Points to Note

In my view, the most significant aspect of this ruling is that it has created conditions for beauty industry workers to actively assert their rights. This raises interest in how employment practices in the beauty industry will change moving forward. If this change takes root, there is a high likelihood of significant improvement in working conditions within the beauty sector.

Hidden Variables to Consider

This ruling will not bring immediate changes to all beauty businesses. Some employers may still attempt to evade worker status by exploiting legal loopholes, making it necessary to monitor and respond to such situations. Additionally, workers will need legal knowledge and support to assert their rights effectively.

Points to Check Now

  • Legal Rights: It is important for beauty industry workers to seek information on how they can assert their rights.
  • Business Practices: Observing how hair designers recognized as workers are entering into employment contracts can also be beneficial.

FAQ

Q1: What rights do hair designers gain when recognized as workers?
A1: Being recognized as workers grants rights such as annual leave, severance pay, and restrictions on dismissal.

Q2: What should I do if I experience unfair dismissal at a hair salon?
A2: If you face unfair dismissal, you can apply for remedy to the labor commission, and it is advisable to seek legal counsel.

Q3: How might employers disguise themselves to evade worker status?
A3: Employers often enter into 'freelance contracts' or register their businesses as separate entities with fewer than five employees to evade worker status.

This ruling has opened up opportunities for beauty industry workers to assert their rights. It is essential to observe how these changes will unfold in practice.
